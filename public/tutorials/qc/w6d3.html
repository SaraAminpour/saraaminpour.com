Fidelity and trace distance

Two numbers for how close two quantum states are, and a beautiful fact: the trace distance tells exactly how well any single measurement can tell them apart.

Warm-up

A guessing game you can't always win

1 min

A friend secretly prepares either ∣0⟩|0\rangle∣0⟩ or ∣+⟩|+\rangle∣+⟩ and hands you the qubit. You may measure it once, any way you like, then guess. How often can you be right? Not always: these states overlap. Today you'll find the exact best score, and the measurement that reaches it.

Big idea

Two distances, one bound

4 min

Fidelity FFF

1 for identical states, 0 for perfectly distinguishable ones. For pure states it's the overlap squared, F=∣⟨ψ∣φ⟩∣2F = |\langle\psi|\varphi\rangle|^2F=∣⟨ψ∣φ⟩∣2; against a mixed state, F=⟨ψ∣ρ∣ψ⟩F = \langle\psi|\rho|\psi\rangleF=⟨ψ∣ρ∣ψ⟩.

Trace distance DDD

D=12∑∣λi∣D = \frac12 \sum |\lambda_i|D=21​∑∣λi​∣, half the sum of the absolute eigenvalues of ρ−σ\rho - \sigmaρ−σ. 0 for identical states, 1 for perfectly distinguishable ones. For a qubit: half the distance between the Bloch arrows' tips.

They are related: for two pure states D=1−FD = \sqrt{1 - F}D=1−F​; in general 1−F≤D≤1−F1 - \sqrt F \le D \le \sqrt{1 - F}1−F​≤D≤1−F​. But they are not the same: ∣0⟩|0\rangle∣0⟩ versus ∣+⟩|+\rangle∣+⟩ and ∣0⟩|0\rangle∣0⟩ versus I/2I/2I/2 both have F=0.5F = 0.5F=0.5, yet D=0.71D = 0.71D=0.71 and D=0.5D = 0.5D=0.5.

The Helstrom bound. Someone sends ρ\rhoρ or σ\sigmaσ, each with probability ½, and you get one copy. The best any measurement can do is

Pcorrect=12 (1+D)P_{\text{correct}} = \tfrac12\,(1 + D)Pcorrect​=21​(1+D)

For a qubit, the optimal measurement is along the axis r⃗ρ−r⃗σ\vec r_\rho - \vec r_\sigmarρ​−rσ​, the direction joining the two Bloch tips: that's where the states differ most. Why: measuring along a unit axis n⃗\vec nn gives "+" with probability 12(1+n⃗⋅r⃗)\frac12(1 + \vec n \cdot \vec r)21​(1+n⋅r), so guessing well succeeds with probability 12+14 ∣n⃗⋅(r⃗ρ−r⃗σ)∣\frac12 + \frac14\,|\vec n \cdot (\vec r_\rho - \vec r_\sigma)|21​+41​∣n⋅(rρ​−rσ​)∣, largest when n⃗\vec nn points along the difference.

So ∣0⟩|0\rangle∣0⟩ versus ∣+⟩|+\rangle∣+⟩: at most 85.4%, never 100%. Non-orthogonal states can't be told apart perfectly. That's not a flaw of our instruments; it's what makes quantum cryptography secure.

Trace distance is distinguishability: one copy, the best measurement, and you're right with probability ½(1 + D).

Fidelity, trace distance and Helstrom in Qiskit

cell 1 · two measures of closeness
import numpy as np
from qiskit import QuantumCircuit
from qiskit.quantum_info import Statevector, DensityMatrix, state_fidelity, Pauli
from qiskit.primitives import StatevectorSampler

def trace_distance(rho, sigma):
    """D = (1/2) * sum of |eigenvalues of rho - sigma|."""
    return 0.5 * np.abs(np.linalg.eigvalsh(DensityMatrix(rho).data - DensityMatrix(sigma).data)).sum()

def bloch(rho):
    return np.array([np.real(DensityMatrix(rho).expectation_value(Pauli(p))) for p in "XYZ"])

zero, one, plus = (DensityMatrix.from_label(s) for s in "01+")
half = DensityMatrix(np.eye(2) / 2)
for name, (a, b) in {"|0> vs |0>": (zero, zero), "|0> vs |+>": (zero, plus),
                     "|0> vs |1>": (zero, one), "|0> vs I/2": (zero, half)}.items():
    F, D = state_fidelity(a, b), trace_distance(a, b)
    print(f"{name}: F = {F:.3f}, D = {D:.3f}, half the distance between the Bloch tips = {0.5 * np.linalg.norm(bloch(a) - bloch(b)):.3f}")

cell 2 · reaching the bound
D = trace_distance(zero, plus)
print(f"Helstrom bound for |0> vs |+>: {(1 + D) / 2:.4f}")

def guess_rate(prepare_A, prepare_B, rotation, shots=20000):
    """Rotate, measure in the 0/1 basis, guess A on 0 and B on 1. Returns the share of right guesses."""
    sampler = StatevectorSampler(seed=7)
    right = 0
    for prepare, correct in [(prepare_A, "0"), (prepare_B, "1")]:
        qc = prepare.copy()
        if rotation:
            qc.ry(rotation, 0)
        qc.measure_all()
        counts = sampler.run([qc], shots=shots).result()[0].data.meas.get_counts()
        right += counts.get(correct, 0)
    return right / (2 * shots)

prep_zero = QuantumCircuit(1)
prep_plus = QuantumCircuit(1)
prep_plus.h(0)
print(f"plain 0/1 measurement      : {guess_rate(prep_zero, prep_plus, 0):.3f}")
print(f"along the axis A - B (RY(pi/4) first): {guess_rate(prep_zero, prep_plus, np.pi / 4):.3f}")

def depolarize(rho, p):
    return DensityMatrix((1 - p) * DensityMatrix(rho).data + p * np.eye(2) / 2)

for p in [0.0, 0.2, 0.5, 0.8]:
    D = trace_distance(depolarize(zero, p), depolarize(plus, p))
    print(f"p = {p:.1f}: trace distance {D:.3f}, best guess rate {(1 + D) / 2:.3f}")
print("Noise shrinks the distance by exactly 1 - p: noisy states look more alike, and every measurement")
print("(including a quantum classifier's) has a harder job.")

Wrap-up

Today in one sentence

1 min
Fidelity (1 = same) and trace distance (0 = same, half the tip-to-tip distance for a qubit) measure closeness; the Helstrom bound says one measurement identifies one of two equally likely states with probability at most ½(1 + D), reached by measuring along their difference.
Quantum link

A quantum classifier for two classes is, in the end, a measurement that tells two families of states apart. So the Helstrom bound is a hard limit on its accuracy for single copies: if the encoded data states of the two classes are close, no training can make the classifier reliable. The Quantum ML tutorial's last week returns to this with "learning from quantum data".

Go deeper (optional)

Nielsen and Chuang, chapter 9 (distance measures for quantum information): trace distance in 9.2.1, fidelity in 9.2.2, and how they relate in 9.2.3. The bound comes from Helstrom's book Quantum Detection and Estimation Theory (1976).
