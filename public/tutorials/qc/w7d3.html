Parameter-shift gradients

No backpropagation on a quantum chip, but two extra runs of the same circuit, a quarter-turn either side, give the exact slope. And it shrugs off shot noise.

Warm-up

Slopes without a look inside

1 min

In ML week 5, backpropagation found every slope of a network in one backward sweep, by storing every value along the way. A quantum chip can't store its amplitudes for later, and looking at them destroys them. So how do we train circuits? With a small miracle that follows from yesterday's sine waves.

Big idea

Half the difference, a quarter-turn away

4 min

Yesterday: with the other knobs fixed, the cost is C(θ)=acos⁡θ+bsin⁡θ+cC(\theta) = a\cos\theta + b\sin\theta + cC(θ)=acosθ+bsinθ+c. Its slope is C′(θ)=−asin⁡θ+bcos⁡θC'(\theta) = -a\sin\theta + b\cos\thetaC′(θ)=−asinθ+bcosθ. Now evaluate the cost a quarter-turn either side and use cos⁡(θ±π2)=∓sin⁡θ\cos(\theta \pm \frac\pi2) = \mp\sin\thetacos(θ±2π​)=∓sinθ, sin⁡(θ±π2)=±cos⁡θ\sin(\theta \pm \frac\pi2) = \pm\cos\thetasin(θ±2π​)=±cosθ:

C(θ+π2)−C(θ−π2)2=−asin⁡θ+bcos⁡θ=C′(θ)\frac{C(\theta + \frac\pi2) - C(\theta - \frac\pi2)}{2} = -a\sin\theta + b\cos\theta = C'(\theta)2C(θ+2π​)−C(θ−2π​)​=−asinθ+bcosθ=C′(θ)

That's the parameter-shift rule: run the same circuit twice, with the knob shifted by +π2+\frac\pi2+2π​ and −π2-\frac\pi2−2π​, and half the difference is the exact slope, not an approximation. (It holds for every gate of the form e−iθP/2e^{-i\theta P/2}e−iθP/2 with a Pauli string PPP, which covers RXR_XRX​, RYR_YRY​, RZR_ZRZ​ and their two-qubit cousins.)

Why not finite differences? C(θ+h)−C(θ−h)2h\frac{C(\theta + h) - C(\theta - h)}{2h}2hC(θ+h)−C(θ−h)​ with a small hhh is approximately right without noise. But on hardware each CCC comes from shots and wobbles by about 1N\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}N​1​. Dividing by a tiny 2h2h2h blows that wobble up: with h=0.01h = 0.01h=0.01 the estimate scatters about 100 times more than the parameter shift's. A big hhh calms the noise but then measures a chord instead of the tangent. The big shift of π2\frac\pi22π​ gets both right.

The price. Two circuits per knob: a gradient over PPP knobs costs 2P2P2P circuits, each run with thousands of shots. Backpropagation pays about one extra pass for all its gradients together. For big quantum models this cost matters.

Shift a knob by ±π/2, run twice, halve the difference: an exact, noise-tolerant slope, at two circuits per knob.

Parameter shift in Qiskit

cell 1 · exact to many digits
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from qiskit import QuantumCircuit
from qiskit.circuit import Parameter
from qiskit.quantum_info import SparsePauliOp
from qiskit.primitives import StatevectorEstimator

theta = Parameter("θ")
one = QuantumCircuit(1)
one.rx(0.9, 0)
one.ry(theta, 0)
Z = SparsePauliOp("Z")
estimator = StatevectorEstimator()
C = lambda t: estimator.run([(one, Z, [t])]).result()[0].data.evs.item()

t0 = 0.6
shift = (C(t0 + np.pi / 2) - C(t0 - np.pi / 2)) / 2
tiny = (C(t0 + 1e-6) - C(t0 - 1e-6)) / 2e-6             # a very small step, fine without shot noise
print(f"parameter shift: {shift:.8f}")
print(f"tiny finite step: {tiny:.8f}")
What it printed in the notebook

cell 2 · every knob, two runs each
H = SparsePauliOp.from_list([("ZZ", 1.0), ("IX", 0.5), ("XI", 0.5)])
t1, t2 = Parameter("θ1"), Parameter("θ2")
two = QuantumCircuit(2)
two.ry(t1, 0)
two.ry(t2, 1)
two.cx(0, 1)
energy = lambda th: estimator.run([(two, H, th)]).result()[0].data.evs.item()

def parameter_shift_gradient(f, theta):
    grad = np.zeros(len(theta))
    for k in range(len(theta)):
        shift = np.zeros(len(theta))
        shift[k] = np.pi / 2
        grad[k] = (f(theta + shift) - f(theta - shift)) / 2
    return grad

theta0 = np.array([0.3, 0.3])
print("gradient:", parameter_shift_gradient(energy, theta0).round(6), " (2 knobs: 4 circuit evaluations)")
What it printed in the notebook
gradient: [0.141161 0.323309]  (2 knobs: 4 circuit evaluations)
cell 3 · the noise race
shots = 1000
noisy = StatevectorEstimator(default_precision=1 / np.sqrt(shots), seed=np.random.default_rng(3))   # a generator: fresh noise every call
Cn = lambda t: noisy.run([(one, Z, [t])]).result()[0].data.evs.item()
truth = shift
ps = [(Cn(t0 + np.pi / 2) - Cn(t0 - np.pi / 2)) / 2 for _ in range(300)]
fd = {h: [(Cn(t0 + h) - Cn(t0 - h)) / (2 * h) for _ in range(300)] for h in [0.01, 0.1]}
print(f"true slope {truth:.3f}")
print(f"parameter shift:        mean {np.mean(ps):+.3f}, spread {np.std(ps):.3f}")
for h, values in fd.items():
    print(f"finite difference h={h}: mean {np.mean(values):+.3f}, spread {np.std(values):.3f}")

Show a solution
one solution
theta = np.array([0.3, 0.3])
energies = [energy(theta)]
for step in range(40):
    theta = theta - 0.2 * parameter_shift_gradient(energy, theta)
    energies.append(energy(theta))
print(f"final energy {energies[-1]:.4f} (ground state {-np.sqrt(2):.4f}) after 40 steps")
print(f"circuit evaluations for the gradients: 40 steps x 2 knobs x 2 shifts = {40 * 2 * 2}")
plt.plot(energies, "o-", ms=4, color="#7b5cfa")
plt.axhline(-np.sqrt(2), color="#17a98c", ls="--", label="ground state")
plt.xlabel("step")
plt.ylabel("energy")
plt.legend()
plt.show()

Wrap-up

Today in one sentence

1 min
Because each knob's cost is a sine wave, half the difference between runs shifted by ±π2\pm\frac\pi2±2π​ is the exact slope; it tolerates shot noise far better than small-step finite differences, at a cost of two circuits per knob.
Quantum link

This is how quantum ML models are trained on real hardware: a classical optimizer such as Adam (ML week 5), fed with parameter-shift gradients. In the Quantum ML tutorial you'll wrap a circuit in PyTorch, so that loss.backward() calls the parameter-shift rule for you.

Go deeper (optional)

Schuld, Bergholm, Gogolin, Izaac and Killoran, "Evaluating analytic gradients on quantum hardware" (Physical Review A, 2019), explains the rule and its generalizations. Mitarai and coauthors, "Quantum circuit learning" (Physical Review A, 2018), used it to train the first circuit-based learning models.
