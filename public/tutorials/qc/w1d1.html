Warm-up

A switch, and something stranger

1 min

Welcome to the Quantum Computing tutorial: seven weeks from a single qubit to circuits that learn, one small lesson each weekday. I'm Qubi, and since I am a qubit, let's start with me.

A bit is like a light switch: it is 0 or 1, and you can look at it as often as you like.

A qubit (that's me!) is stranger. Before you look, I carry two numbers that decide how likely you are to see 0 or 1. When you look, you see just one bit, and the two numbers are gone. Today you'll learn what those two numbers are.

Big idea

Two amplitudes

4 min

A qubit's state is written as a mix of the two possible outcomes:

∣ψ⟩=α ∣0⟩+β ∣1⟩|\psi\rangle = \alpha\,|0\rangle + \beta\,|1\rangle∣ψ⟩=α∣0⟩+β∣1⟩

The funny brackets are ket notation. They are just name tags for vectors: ∣0⟩|0\rangle∣0⟩ means (10)\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\end{pmatrix}(10​) and ∣1⟩|1\rangle∣1⟩ means (01)\begin{pmatrix}0\\1\end{pmatrix}(01​). So the state is simply a column of two numbers:

∣ψ⟩=(αβ)|\psi\rangle = \begin{pmatrix}\alpha\\ \beta\end{pmatrix}∣ψ⟩=(αβ​)

α\alphaα and β\betaβ are called amplitudes. They can be negative, and in general they are complex numbers. They are not probabilities yet. To get probabilities you square their size. That is the Born rule:

P(0)=∣α∣2P(1)=∣β∣2∣α∣2+∣β∣2=1P(0) = |\alpha|^2 \qquad P(1) = |\beta|^2 \qquad |\alpha|^2 + |\beta|^2 = 1P(0)=∣α∣2P(1)=∣β∣2∣α∣2+∣β∣2=1

The last condition is called normalization: the two probabilities must add up to 1. If both amplitudes are real, that means the point (α,β)(\alpha, \beta)(α,β) sits on a circle of radius 1. That's the picture you'll play with next.

A coin (probabilistic bit)

Described by two probabilities (p0,p1)(p_0, p_1)(p0​,p1​), both between 0 and 1, adding up to 1: p0+p1=1p_0 + p_1 = 1p0​+p1​=1.

A qubit

Described by two amplitudes (α,β)(\alpha, \beta)(α,β) whose squares add up to 1. Amplitudes can be negative, and that small difference is what makes quantum computing powerful (week 3).

What does measuring do?

Measuring gives one outcome, 0 or 1, at random with those probabilities. Afterwards the qubit is whatever you saw: ∣0⟩|0\rangle∣0⟩ or ∣1⟩|1\rangle∣1⟩. To learn α\alphaα and β\betaβ you need many fresh copies of the same state and many measurements. Physicists call each repetition a shot.

Things to notice

Put the dot at the top: β=1\beta = 1β=1, so you always measure 1. Put it exactly halfway between the axes: that is the famous ∣+⟩|+\rangle∣+⟩ state, a fair 50/50 coin. Now drag it to the lower right: β\betaβ is negative but the odds are the same! Signs are invisible to a single measurement. In week 3 you'll see where they matter.

First with plain NumPy: a state is just an array.

cell 1 · amplitudes to probabilities
import numpy as np

psi = np.array([np.sqrt(0.7), np.sqrt(0.3)])   # (alpha, beta)
probs = np.abs(psi) ** 2                       # Born rule: probability = |amplitude|^2

print("amplitudes   :", psi.round(3))
print("probabilities:", probs.round(3))
print("sum          :", probs.sum().round(3))

Which of these are real quantum states?

cell 2 · normalization check
def is_valid(state):
    return np.isclose(np.sum(np.abs(state) ** 2), 1.0)

candidates = {
    "(1, 0)": np.array([1, 0]),
    "(0.6, 0.8)": np.array([0.6, 0.8]),
    "(0.6, -0.8)": np.array([0.6, -0.8]),
    "(0.5, 0.5)": np.array([0.5, 0.5]),
    "(1, i)/sqrt(2)": np.array([1, 1j]) / np.sqrt(2),
}
for name, state in candidates.items():
    print(f"{name:>15}  ->  {'valid' if is_valid(state) else 'not normalized'}")

Now Qiskit's Statevector, which can also simulate measurements:

cell 3 · Statevector
from qiskit.quantum_info import Statevector

sv = Statevector([np.sqrt(0.7), np.sqrt(0.3)])
print(sv)
print("probabilities:", sv.probabilities().round(3))   # [P(0), P(1)]

sv.seed(7)                                  # fixed seed so the "random" result repeats
print("10 measurements:", sv.sample_memory(10))

cell 4 · more shots, better estimates
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 3, figsize=(9.5, 3.2), sharey=True, layout="constrained")
for ax, shots in zip(axes, [10, 100, 10000]):
    sv.seed(7)
    counts = sv.sample_counts(shots)
    freq = [counts.get("0", 0) / shots, counts.get("1", 0) / shots]
    ax.bar(["0", "1"], freq, color=["#5577f5", "#ff8a50"], width=0.6)
    ax.axhline(0.7, color="#5577f5", ls="--", lw=1)
    ax.axhline(0.3, color="#ff8a50", ls="--", lw=1)
    ax.set_title(f"{shots} shots")
axes[0].set_ylabel("fraction of shots")
plt.show()
What it printed in the notebook

With 10 shots you got 5 zeros and 5 ones, far from 70/30. With 10,000 shots the bars sit right on the dashed lines. That is why quantum experiments repeat everything thousands of times.

The length of a vector is ∣v0∣2+∣v1∣2\sqrt{|v_0|^2 + |v_1|^2}∣v0​∣2+∣v1​∣2​, which NumPy computes with np.linalg.norm(v). Divide vvv by it.

Show a solution
one solution
def normalize(v):
    v = np.asarray(v, dtype=complex)
    return v / np.linalg.norm(v)

for v in [[3, 4], [1, 1], [1, 1j]]:
    state = normalize(v)
    probs = np.abs(state) ** 2
    print(f"{str(v):>8} -> amplitudes {state.round(3)}  probabilities {probs.round(2)}  valid? {is_valid(state)}")
print("(1, 1) and (1, i) give the same 50/50 odds, yet they are different states. Tomorrow's lesson is about that difference.")

Wrap-up

Today in one sentence

1 min
A qubit is a pair of amplitudes (α,β)(\alpha, \beta)(α,β). Square them to get the probabilities of 0 and 1, which must add up to 1. One measurement gives one random bit; many shots reveal the probabilities.
Quantum link

This is how a quantum model gives an answer. A quantum classifier ends with a measurement, and a probability such as P(1)P(1)P(1) is read as "how likely this input is class 1". Because it comes from shots, it is always an estimate, which is one reason quantum ML needs care with noise.
